About my research

Connecting river dynamics to carbon fate

My research asks a fundamental question: what controls the fate of terrestrial organic carbon as it is transported through dynamic sedimentary landscapes? Carbon delivered to a river does not simply move from source to ocean. Its fate emerges from the interaction among sediment transport, geomorphic storage and reworking, and biogeochemical degradation. Understanding whether carbon is ultimately oxidized, transiently stored, or exported therefore requires resolving both how it moves and what happens during the time it spends within the landscape.

A major focus of my work is the coupling between organic carbon and fine sediment. Mud can aggregate into flocs that settle much faster than their constituent primary particles, shortening characteristic transport distances and creating opportunities for sediment-associated organic carbon to enter depositional environments rather than remaining continuously in downstream transport. Floodplains are one such environment, but they are not permanent sinks: rivers continually construct and erode them through channel migration and sediment deposition. Geomorphic reworking therefore provides a characteristic window of storage—from deposition until subsequent erosion—during which organic carbon can either persist or be transformed.

This leads to the biogeochemical part of the problem: what determines the fate of carbon during that storage interval? My work in discontinuous-permafrost floodplains shows that organic carbon can persist for millennia in buried deposits, and that this persistence cannot be understood from temperature or frozen-ground state alone. Anaerobic conditions can strongly limit degradation, while channel migration eventually remobilizes stored sediment and carbon. The climatic significance of riverine carbon therefore depends not simply on flux or instantaneous stock, but on the competition among deposition, storage duration, degradation, and geomorphic recycling.

I approach this problem by connecting sediment-transport and landscape-evolution processes with geochemical measurements. My work combines field observations, remote sensing, sedimentology, radiocarbon, and ramped pyrolysis–oxidation to constrain carbon sources, transport and storage timescales, and reactivity. Across river systems and ultimately through comparative efforts such as MOREPOC, my goal is to develop a process-based understanding of how transport, transient storage, degradation, and remobilization together determine the fate of terrestrial organic carbon from source to sink.

Published dataset

MOREPOC: a global map of riverine particulate organic carbon

MOREPOC v1.1 is an open, georeferenced database of suspended particulate organic carbon in global rivers. The map highlights catchments I have worked in by default; the grouped river/location-level MOREPOC data layer can be turned on when visitors want to explore values.
