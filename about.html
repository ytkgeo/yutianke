Biography

From river sediment grains to Earth-system carbon dynamics

My research asks a single question: how do the Earth surface processes that erode, transport, and store sediment determine the fate of mobilized organic carbon? Erosion can expose petrogenic organic carbon to oxidation and release geologic CO2 to the atmosphere, or it can simply redistribute carbon through repeated sedimentary cycles with little net climatic effect. Biospheric carbon may be oxidized during transport, stored temporarily within floodplains, or buried in long-lived sedimentary reservoirs. The balance among these competing pathways governs how landscapes interact with the atmospheric carbon reservoir.

I treat a catchment as one connected system rather than a set of separate subjects. When a hillslope fails or a riverbank collapses, fresh mineral surfaces and rock-derived carbon are exposed to oxidation at the same moment that soil carbon accumulated over centuries to millennia is mobilized. What happens next is set by two quantities: how long carbon stays in a given environment, and how readily it is oxidized once there. I use the physical understanding of Earth surface processes to constrain the first, and carbon chemistry to constrain the second.

My training spans both sides of that problem. I began in geological engineering, working on loess landslides and slope stability, and moved into carbon cycle geochemistry during my doctoral and postdoctoral research. My fieldwork spans the Yangtze, Yellow, Pearl, Amur, and Yukon basins. Looking ahead, I plan to establish the Earth Surface and Carbon Dynamics Laboratory, pairing field campaigns with ramped pyrolysis–oxidation, radiocarbon, and large environmental datasets to quantify how a moving land surface regulates carbon storage and release.

Storage, degradation, and turnover in river systems

River corridors are the interface between continental erosion and long-term burial. Floodplains act as reactors in which material delivered from hillslopes is stored, altered, and re-entrained before reaching the ocean. Residence time in that reactor — set largely by channel migration and overbank deposition — is a dominant control on whether carbon survives.

Flocculation and the physical control on carbon exposure

Organic carbon does not move independently through river systems; it travels with fine sediment, whose behaviour is fundamentally altered by flocculation. Binding mineral particles into aggregates changes settling velocity and deposition pathways, and floc interiors may act as protected microenvironments that limit oxidation during transport.

Disturbance and the resetting of carbon fate

Landslides, riverbank collapse, thaw slumping, lake drainage, and flood scour rapidly mobilize sediment and organic carbon. Rather than treating these as isolated hazards, I study them as Earth surface processes that initiate a new trajectory of carbon transport, transformation, and storage.

Research vision

Future directions in Earth surface carbon science

Each theme is an entry point into the same sequence of erosion, transport, storage, and burial, and each provides quantities the others require. These are the questions I am pursuing next.

Floodplain storage

How much carbon, and for how long?

No global estimate of floodplain organic carbon stocks and residence times with quantified uncertainty yet exists, which is why floodplains enter land surface models as fitted terms. Producing one is a tractable and high-value target.

Flocculation

A transferable predictor of persistence

Thermal activation energy spectra, mineral surface area, and deposit age may together predict how long carbon survives in a given sedimentary setting, replacing site-by-site calibration with a measurable, transferable parameter.

Disturbance

Disturbance under a warming climate

Linking event-scale measurements to the storage and transport frameworks above, to determine how changing disturbance frequency alters carbon budgets — and whether carbon itself feeds back on erodibility and landscape evolution.
